2518 South Street · Philadelphia

A lumber yard failed.
Thirty-eight houses went up.
One of them is yours.

Before this ground was your house, it was part of a lumber yard, and the yard held this corner for nearly ninety years.

Before that it was the northern edge of the United States Naval Asylum, bought in 1826 with money deducted from sailors’ wages.

Before that it was a Quaker family’s country plantation. The Pembertons held it, and the mapmakers simply lettered it Pemberton.

Before that it ran back through a line of colonial owners to a patent the Penns granted in 1684 to Lasse Cock, sometimes written Lacy, the Swede who interpreted for William Penn at his treaties with the Lenape.

Before that it was Swedish farmland. The earliest conveyance anybody has recited is Peter Matson to Matthias Holsten, May 5, 1672, ten years before William Penn arrived.

And before that it was Lenape ground. The English wrote down what happened in the 1680s as purchase, with deeds signed and goods handed over. The Lenape were agreeing to share the use of land they did not regard as a thing that could be sold outright. No account of their side of it survives in a form we can read. What survives is the name. Passyunk is Pachsegink, “in the valley”: the low land between the two rivers.

One caution about the chain above. Everything from the federal purchase of 1826 forward is documented for this parcel, deed by deed. Everything before it is inference. The seventeenth- and eighteenth-century links come from a chain recited to the Board of Property for a 102-acre tract in Passyunk, and we have not proved it is this ground. It almost certainly is. Lasse Cock was already living on a hundred acres at Passyunk in 1678, the acreages agree, and the same surnames turn up on the early maps around this block, but almost certainly is not the same as proved.

1788 – 1840

The streets were drawn a hundred years before the house

In 1787 the General Assembly appointed commissioners to lay out new roads through the townships below the city, and John Hills drew the result the following year. Your ground is in that drawing, as farmland with a road drawn across it and nothing built.

The commissioners came back forty-four years later with a surveyor. John Thomson laid out the streets of Passyunk Township in 1832, from the Schuylkill to the Moyamensing line and from Cedar Street south. He drew the Naval Asylum, an old glass house by the river, and a dozen buildings with their owners’ names beside them. The report was filed that December and confirmed the following April.

The sheet’s own title: Plan of the streets in the Township of Passyunk between Schuylkill River and the Moyamensing Township line and between Cedar Street and the Buck Road laid out in 1832. Lemuel Paynter, William G. Alexander, Fredrick G. Wolbert, Commissioners. John Thomson, Surveyor.

One thing on that sheet is worth stopping at. Shippen Street is lettered, and directly above it, in pencil and a later hand, somebody has written BAINBRIDGE. The Navy’s deed of 1826 had called that line “Shippen street continued.” The renaming is written onto the drawing as it happened.

Then the grades. Under an Act of Assembly of June 21, 1839, Samuel H. Kneass figured the curb height of every corner in the northern section of Passyunk, together with the water courses and the culverts that carried them. The commissioners approved it in October 1840. On the city side of Cedar Street they had been calculating drainage for a sewer since about 1815, when there was still almost nothing here to drain.

So the order runs backwards from what you would expect. First the street on paper, then its height above the water, then the culverts under it, and only then, half a century later, a lumber yard and a row of houses. The grid arrived and waited.

1826 – 1857

Federal ground, bought with sailors’ wages

The ground under those paper streets already had an owner. The Navy had bought about twenty-three acres here in 1826 for its asylum: a home for sailors too old or too badly hurt to go back to sea. It was paid for out of their own money: twenty cents a month, deducted from every seaman’s pay since 1799. The men who lived in it had bought it, a fifth of a dollar at a time.

When the government bought, Bainbridge Street did not exist west of Gray’s Ferry Road. Years later the city did continue it, driving the street west through ground the Navy owned, and the cut left a long thin strip stranded on the far side. By 1857 the Navy had land it could not use, on the wrong side of a public street, and an Act of Congress authorizing its sale. This house stands on that strip. Whether the severance caused the sale or only smoothed the way, the statute is what made your house possible.

Congress, March 3, 1857: the Secretary of the Navy may sell “the plat of ground cut off from the asylum by Shippen street… the proceeds to be carried to the credit of the Naval Hospital fund, from which fund they were purchased.”

1860 – 1870

The Maules move onto ground the Navy still owns

The Maules were Quakers out of Delaware County, in the lumber trade. The firm dates from a notice in the Public Ledger of February 6, 1845: Israel Maule has this day associated with him Edward Maule, in the lumber business, which will hereafter be conducted at the old stand, Schuylkill Sixth Street, between Spruce and Pine, under the firm of Maule & Brother. (Schuylkill Sixth is Seventeenth Street.)

Their cousin Henry moved the yard out to the city’s old southern edge around 1861, and the name stretched to Maule, Brother & Co., so for the whole life of this firm the “& Co.” is Cousin Henry.

McElroy’s city directory puts Maule, Brother & Co. on South Street in 1860 and at South and Twenty-fifth in 1861. By the spring of 1865 they were advertising in the Inquirer almost daily.

Not one advertisement but five, stacked under a standing head reading LUMBER., each signed the same way. ROSEWOOD PLANK AND LOGS · SEGAR BOX MANUFACTURERS · SPANISH CEDAR BOX BOARDS · COFFIN BOARDS · ALBANY LUMBER OF EVERY DESCRIPTION. And under every one of them, MAULE BROTHER & CO., No. 2500 SOUTH Street.

Smedley’s atlas of 1862 letters this parcel Lumber Yard and dimensions it at 618 ft 9½ in of frontage. The Navy did not sell until January 1870, so the yard is on the map eight years before its owner is, and nothing we have found says on what terms it stood there.

January 8, 1870

After ten years on it, they buy the ground

At public auction at the Philadelphia Exchange, Edward Maule took lots 3 to 16 and 18 for $13,200, and the patent was sealed at Washington that May, signed by President Grant. This is not a speculator buying at a government sale: the firm had been trading from this corner since the 1860 directory, and the auction only made them owners of a yard they were already running.

1878 – February 1879

Israel Junior dies, and the ground is split

To see why one death could break the firm, go back to 1813. That autumn, nine weeks apart at Pine Street Meeting, two Maule half-brothers married two Price sisters: Caleb to Lavinia in September, Israel Senior to Ann in December. Edward and Henry came out of those two marriages, their mothers sisters and their fathers half-brothers. Not a line, but a knot.

Israel Junior — the Israel of the 1845 notice, son of an Israel who died in 1828 — was taken suddenly on April 21, 1878, by what the record calls apoplexy and we would call a stroke, and buried from his brother Edward’s house at 1409 Spruce Street. He had owned about half the business, and his widow Martha Nicholson withdrew his share. The money was not to be found anywhere else, so the ground had to be divided to settle her out. She outlived the firm, the foreclosure and Edward himself.

It was divided in February 1879. Henry kept the corner 101 feet and went on selling lumber from it. Edward took the 161 feet beyond, and with it the two mortgages Israel had put on the ground in 1873 and 1878.

The meeting records add one more thing. Both cousins had been put out of the Meeting. “Edward dis for mou” and “Henry dis for mou”, disowned for marrying out of unity. Israel Junior is not among those put out; the two men left running this yard were.

Henry survived all of it. He kept his 101 feet, and from 1877 began selling seeds out of the lumber business. His son William Henry Maule took the seeds over in 1882 and built a national mail-order house on them, posting free seed packets to any farmer with a mailbox. By the time his father died there were 560,000 regular customers and a partnership with W. Atlee Burpee. One of the largest seed companies in the world started in the lumber yard two doors from your house. The other son, Charles, took the lumber and held this corner until he died in 1950.

October 24, 1883 · December 1, 1884

The firm suspends, and the creditors turn out to be family

Five years after the partition, on October 24, 1883, Maule, Bro. & Co. suspended, owing about $150,000 against assets described as “largely real estate, at present unproductive”: a polite way of saying: this yard. The newspaper traced it back to the money that had left with Israel Junior’s widow.

The creditors’ list is a family document. Edward’s nephew Evert P. Maule had been financed by his uncle for years and owed $12,000 to the firm and $17,000 to Edward personally. The largest creditor was not a bank but Mrs Mary A. Barcroft, a widow holding $88,213.60 in confessed judgments, more than half the stated liabilities. Another $5,600 was held by Lavinia Maule, Edward’s unmarried sister, sixty-three and living in his house.

Count them: Israel’s widow, who withdrew his capital; Mrs Barcroft, who held the judgments; and Edward’s sister Lavinia. Two widows and a spinster sister held the paper that broke the family firm and put this ground under the hammer.

Thirteen months later, at four in the afternoon on December 1, 1884, the sheriff exposed Edward’s 161 feet at the New County Court House under a writ of levari facias, which is how mortgaged land is seized and sold after default. He did not sell this ground. He was foreclosed on it.

The advertisement prints a figure between the case number and the plaintiff’s attorney: “C. P. No. 2, Sept. Term, 1884, No. 225. $26,762.50. McKeehan.” That is the debt to be satisfied, not a price anybody paid. What happened at four o’clock is not in the newspaper.

But the sale did not fail. The lender bought it. Fidelity Insurance & Trust held the mortgage, and a lender can bid at its own foreclosure without paying anything, offsetting the price against the debt. Five years later, on February 25, 1888, Fidelity sold the whole 161 feet to Forbes for $25,000, less than it was owed. That is a bank clearing a bad loan, not somebody who has found a bargain.

February – March 1888

A builder cuts a street through it and names it after himself

On February 25, 1888 Fidelity sold the 161 feet to William Forbes for $25,000. The directories call him an operative builder: a man who put up houses on his own account, to sell. Within a month he had driven a new street through the middle of the yard and laid out thirty-eight lots, each charged with a perpetual ground rent, a Philadelphia habit by which the buyer owns the house but pays the ground’s owner a fixed sum every year, forever: $90 a year on this lot, $60 on a fourteen-foot lot around the corner.

He called the street Forbes Street. By 1901 the city had renamed it Bambrey. His name on it lasted about a dozen years.

Count the frontages on South Street: four at 16 feet and four at 15 feet 6 inches, 64 and 62, and the new street at 35. 64 plus 62 plus 35 is 161 feet exactly, the width of the parcel the sheriff sold.

May 14, 1888

Eight houses, two with shops, and yours is the third

The permit, printed in a trade weekly that week: “Wm Forbes, eight 3-sty dwgs, two with stores, S s South st, bet 25th and 26th sts, 16x44.”

The City of Philadelphia’s property record gives the build date as 1915. The permit is dated May 14, 1888. The city is twenty-seven years late, and its own data flags the year as an estimate, so it is not really claiming otherwise.

Yours is the third along: 2522 on the corner of the new street, then 2520, then you, with 2516 beyond you against the yard. In Forbes’s lot schedule of March 1888 that is 116 feet 6 inches west of Twenty-fifth, 15 feet 6 inches wide, 58 feet deep, ground rent $90 a year, still in your deed, word for word.

1890 – 1924

The builder dies before anyone moves in

William Forbes died on March 19, 1890, aged forty-nine, of hypertrophy and partial cirrhosis of the liver. He was a carpenter and builder, born in Ireland, and he was buried at Mount Moriah. His widow Elizabeth A. Forbes and the others named in his will held the row for thirty-four years and never lived in it. It took in lodgers from the start.

The first person we can name inside this house is John Liveny, a horsecar conductor of twenty-eight, and we know him only because in July 1890 he crushed his ankle jumping from his own car.

By 1892 it is a boarding house under a named keeper, Bridget Nolan, letting rooms to single working men: George Patton in 1892, John McCullough and William McMenamin in 1893, each entered in the directory as a driver. Many of them worked across the road. The horsecar depot on the north side of South Street filled two blocks with car houses and stables, and a fire-insurance surveyor counted 179 horses in it in 1882.

Their trade was already finished. Philadelphia’s first electric cars ran on December 15, 1892; the last horse car ran in 1897, and that same year the Lombard and South Street Passenger Railway was leased away and ceased to exist. The company ended. Its purpose did not. The barn opposite became the Philadelphia Rapid Transit Company’s car house, and stayed in that use until The Philadelphia School, a private school, bought it in the 1970s.

There is no census of this house in 1890, and there never will be. The 1890 population schedules for Pennsylvania were destroyed: burned, then soaked by the firemen, in a Washington basement on January 10, 1921, and thrown out as waste in 1935. Fragments survive for eleven states and the District of Columbia; Pennsylvania is not among them. The record that should have listed everyone here two years after the house was built no longer exists.

October 8, 1906

A child from this house with a penny for sweets

Then the electric cars that had replaced the horses killed a child from this house.

Bessie Robinson was three years and seven months old. The Robinsons rented here; her father, Thomas, printed wallpaper. Her mother had dressed her that morning and given her a penny for sweets at a store across the road. A South Street car came down the hill from Twenty-fourth without slowing. A fireman riding the front of it, Hoseman William Donahue of Engine Company No. 5, leaned out to catch her and went into the street himself. The motorman braked too late. Her mother heard it from the doorway, and when she came out and learned whose child it was, fainted in the street.

The Inquirer ran her photograph on the front page the next morning under Child Crushed to Death Under a Speeding Car. She was the sixth child killed by a trolley in the lower part of the city inside a month. The crew was arrested and the coroner promised a thorough investigation.

The records close it in two lines. The coroner’s certificate, no. 93428, gives the place of death as 25th & South Sts. and the hour as 12:20 PM. The death notice gives the address: “the funeral, on Thursday, at 2 P.M., from her residence, 2518 South st. Interment Moriah Cemetery.” There is no stone for her at Mount Moriah, and no index to look in.

December 9, 1924

An Irish widow buys it, in her own name, alone

Mary Maloney was born in Ireland in July 1873 and landed here in 1888, aged fifteen. On December 14, 1893 she married James Stevens at Philadelphia, both of them lodging at the same Locust Street address; the clerk wrote them down as Stephens and Malone, and James signed the license with his mark. The 1900 census counts three children born to her and two of them living. Four names come off the sheets over the years — James, Agnes, Bridget and John — though the clerks who made her Stephens and Malone may also have made Agnes into Bridget.

The license calls James a laborer. By the end he was cutting stone, and on April 1, 1911 he died at 2406 Panama Street, aged thirty-seven, of consumption, which is what they called tuberculosis. His certificate names his own parents, Patrick Stevens and Bridget Corcoran of Ireland, the only pair of grandparents this search has turned up. Mary signed it as informant. She was thirty-seven herself, with three children at home, and she was naturalized in her own right five years later, which a married woman could not do and a widow could.

In January 1920 she is not on this block. She is a few streets north-east, at 2411 Delancey Street, in her son-in-law’s household: a widow with a bed in somebody else’s house and no title to anything.

Four years later she buys 2518 in her own name. The registry book keeps one line to a lot, and on the sheet below, the lots either side of hers are entered as couples, with his wife interlined above the second name in a smaller hand. Line 160 never carries a couple. It runs Elizabeth Forbes, then Mary Stevens, then Mary Stevens and Sara Donahue, then Sara Donahue, then Agnes Huff. Where a fifty-one-year-old widowed housekeeper found the price of a house is not recorded anywhere we have looked.

One thing the record refuses to settle: where in Ireland she was born. Four documents give three answers. “Ireland” in 1920, “Irish Free State” in 1930, “N. Ireland” in 1940, and “Ireland” again on her death certificate in 1949. The first shift is only the map moving: in 1920 there was one Ireland to name, and by 1930 there were two. But 1930 and 1940 contradict each other outright. Both censuses allowed either answer, and the Free State was the twenty-six counties while Northern Ireland was the six that stayed British. Somebody was wrong, or nobody knew.

The certificate is no help on her parents either: her father is entered as Michael Maloney, and against her mother’s maiden name the 1949 clerk wrote “Unknown.” Agnes was standing in her mother’s house, answering questions about her own grandmother, and could not supply the name.

June 14 – 19, 1920

Mary’s son John shot as a deserter

Four and a half years before she signed for this house, Mary’s son John was shot dead in the street by a sergeant of his own company. The Inquirer put it on the front page.

The enumerator who found her at 2411 Delancey Street in January 1920 wrote the household down in six lines, and mother and son are both in it: Stephens, Mary on line 68 as mother-in-law, Stephens, John J. on line 71 as brother-in-law. It is the only sheet the two of them share, and it is five months before he was killed.

He had enlisted at sixteen by telling them he was nineteen, and he was in Balloon Company No. 18. On Monday, June 14, 1920, on the Parkway at Twenty-second Street, he was driving a motor truck when a sergeant of his own company called on him to stop and then shot him through both lungs. He lived two days. He was conscious long enough to give a magistrate a statement — he had not heard the command, and could not have stopped the truck in the time he had — and long enough to talk to his wife, a girl the death notice calls Marie, whom he had married a few months before. He was seventeen. So was she.

The word the army used for him was deserter, and the paper carried two irreconcilable accounts of how he earned it. His mother's: he was sent to a camp hospital ill, given leave home, wrote asking for the leave to be extended because the family needed him, and never got an answer. His lieutenant's: he was awaiting trial for striking a non-commissioned officer, was taken to Fort McHenry as a prisoner, and escaped, and the arrest order went out because he had boasted “I would like to see anybody get me.” Both ran on the same day, in the same column.

Mary had put a blessed candle in his right hand just before he died. Then she stood at her own front door on Delancey Street and said the only words of hers anybody ever wrote down.

“I would rather they did not punish the man who shot my son. I feel the mother of the other boy must be considered. It will not bring my son back or do any good to prosecute the soldier who shot him, and I do not wish another mother to feel the way I do.”

She said one thing more, and the paper printed it without comment. Grover Cleveland Bergdoll was a Philadelphia brewing heir who had spent the war hiding from conscription on his mother’s estate, and by 1920 he was the most notorious draft evader in the country. When he ran from the men guarding him, nobody fired.

“Neither of the sergeants in charge of Bergdoll fired at him when he ran away from them. But he has money. My boy was poor.”

Nothing came of any of it. Sergeant Roger Gay had been cleared by his own company’s board before the inquest even sat, and sent to Aberdeen to await court-martial. The jury found that Stevens died of a gunshot wound to the lung inflicted by Gay; the District Attorney spoke to Washington and handed the case to the army. Whether Gay was ever tried is not established, and no civilian prosecution followed.

Well, one thing did come of it. Agnes and Alexander Huff had a son later that year and called him John J. Huff. He was named for an uncle shot dead at seventeen that June, and he grew up in this house.

1930 · 1940

Nine people in fifteen feet six inches

In April 1930 the enumerator counts nine in this house. Note who is at the top of the list. Mary is head of the household and the Huffs are the lodgers: Alexander, a grocery clerk; Agnes his wife, who is Mary’s daughter; and six children, the youngest two. She had taken them in, not the other way round this time. Nine people in a house fifty-eight feet deep, over three floors and a basement.

Two things on that sheet are wrong. The tenure column says the house is rented, at $60 a month, though Mary had held the deed since December 1924. And the surname is written STEVENSON. A census records what somebody said at a door and what the man with the clipboard thought he heard. It is not a title search.

Ten years later the errors do not clear up. They change places. In April 1940 the spelling is right, STEVENS, and the tenure column finally says O, owned, valued at $4,000. But Alexander Huff is now at the top as head of the household, and Mary is the last line of it: mother-in-law, 69, widowed, same house since 1935. She had not moved and she had not sold. The one sheet that finally calls this house owned falsely states it was owned by her son-in-law.

Mary Stevens held the deed from December 1924 until she died at home in April 1949. In 1930 the census called her house rented; in 1940 it called it owned, at the top of a list she is at the bottom of. No enumerator ever wrote her down as the owner of anything.

April 27, 1949, 5:15 AM

She dies in the house she bought

Mary Stevens died at a quarter past five in the morning, at home, aged seventy-five. Her daughter Agnes signed the certificate as informant and gave her own address as the same house. Where the certificate asks for the name of the hospital or institution, the clerk wrote 2518 South Street, because there wasn’t one.

The title had been arranged, years earlier, for exactly this morning. At some point Mary had put a second name beside her own: Sara Donahue, whose relationship to her we still cannot determine. The two of them held the house as joint tenants with right of survivorship. That is not something that happens to you; it is a form two people have to choose. Survivorship passes the whole house to the survivor the instant the other dies: no will, no probate, no court, nothing for anybody to contest.

It worked. On the morning Mary died, Sara Donahue owned this house outright and Agnes owned nothing; six months and nine days later Sara conveyed it to Agnes. But that is the shape of the thing, not an explanation. Mary did not leave this house to her daughter. She arranged for it to pass to a woman we cannot identify, who then transferred it to her daughter. Two steps where one would have done.

Why is genuinely open. Perhaps Sara had lent the money and the survivorship was security. Perhaps two widows were insuring each other. Perhaps the family had been buying the house on installments through the 1930s (the 1940 census calls them owners nine years before any deed) and November 1949 is when Agnes made the last payment. The document that would decide it is not online: deed C.J.P. 2479 p. 221. Its consideration is the whole test. A dollar means a family arrangement finishing; a market price means Agnes bought her mother’s house from a stranger.

What no document gives is who Sara Donahue was. She holds half of this house for years, takes the whole of it in a morning, gives it away by winter, and no record we have found states any relationship between the two women.

November 5, 1949 – 1984

Agnes takes it alone, and keeps it thirty-five years

On November 5, 1949 Sara Donahue conveyed 2518 South Street to Agnes M. Huff, the daughter who had come into this house nineteen years earlier as a lodger in her own mother’s household. What she paid is not on the registry line.

She took it in her own name alone, and five months later the 1950 census says why. The household at 2518 is down to two: Agnes, 49, head, marked separated, working as a cheese packer for a milk company, and her son, Joseph A., 21, packing cartons. Alexander is one block west, lodging at 2620 South Street, also marked separated.

Halfway through those thirty-five years the Inquirer came to look at the neighborhood. On July 9, 1967, under the headline Progress Brings Changes for Schuylkill Section, it described a place two blocks by two around Twenty-sixth and South, a thousand residents, “almost all Irish Catholic,” where you couldn’t throw a rock for fear of hitting a relative, where the corner bar was Callahan’s and the Fourth of July still had a block party. The reporter’s title for it was The Death of a Neighborhood: Black families were moving up from the south, Center City buyers were turning row houses on the north edge into “plush townhouses,” a luxury high-rise was proposed for Twenty-seventh and Lombard, and the city was talking about a spur of the Crosstown Expressway to the South Street bridge, which would have taken the lower half of Schuylkill with it. Three days earlier Cecil B. Moore had held his swim-in at the little city pool at Twenty-sixth and South, which the Irish of Schuylkill had kept to themselves for years. Agnes, sixty-six, eighteen years into her tenure, was one of the thousand.

He never came back. He died in December 1972 and was buried at Holy Cross; Agnes died on January 13, 1984 and was buried at Holy Sepulchre. So the longest single ownership in this house’s history belongs to a separated woman who bought her mother’s house and kept it for thirty-five years. From Forbes’s death in 1890 to hers, ninety-four years, this house did not belong to a man for a single day.

1962 → today

Seven of the eight are still standing

The yard did not outlive the houses after all. Sanborn drew fire insurance atlases: a surveyor walked the block and recorded every building, what it was made of and what might burn in it. Their sheet for this block, corrected to December 1950, shows the ground beside your houses blank. No sheds, no piles, no name, just enclosed by 6 ft. wire fence. Charles Maule died that year with the corner already cleared. The city went on lettering it Lumber Yard until at least 1962, which is a fact about maps rather than about the yard.

2516 came down between 1962, when a map still shows it, and 1996, when an aerial does not. The registry book gives it two owners after the Forbes estate, a couple named Marsden in 1923 and a Harry Friedman by sheriff’s sale in the 1930s, and then the line goes blank. In 2013 a four-story condominium went up across the old yard and 2516’s lot together, taking the numbers 2502 to 2516 for its units.

Every street around this block has been renamed at least once. Cedar became South, Shippen became Bainbridge, Meredith became Naudain, and Forbes, which the builder named after himself, became Bambrey. The fifth is the one worth stopping on.

Barnwell became Taney, and Taney Street carried the name of Roger B. Taney, the Chief Justice who wrote Dred Scott in 1857, the ruling that held no Black person could be a citizen of the United States. In 2024 the city renamed it for Caroline LeCount, a Black schoolteacher who in 1867 had a conductor arrested for refusing her a seat on a Philadelphia streetcar. A street named for the man who denied her citizenship now carries the name of the woman who took a streetcar company to court.

The lumber yard held the corner for nearly ninety years; the eight houses arrived in a single season; one of them is gone; and a condominium now runs from Twenty-fifth Street up to this house’s east party wall.

Two dates here are honest gaps, not facts. The yard is drawn as a yard through 1962 because the city’s land-use map still letters it LUMBER YARD that year; when it actually closed is not established. 2516 is drawn standing in 1962 and gone in 1996, because those are the two pictures we have, the land-use map and the aerial, and nothing yet narrows those thirty-four years. Only the condominium of 2013 is dated from a record rather than a photograph.

Women owned this house for about a hundred and eight of its hundred and thirty-eight years.

The three longest holdings in its history are all women’s, and for ninety-four unbroken years, from the builder’s death in 1890 to Agnes Huff’s in 1984, it never once belonged to a man.

What the record keeps, and what it loses

This house is legible because of other people’s disasters. A firm failed, so the ground came up for sale. A builder died, so his widow’s name went onto thirty-eight deeds. A conductor crushed his ankle jumping off a moving car, so we know somebody was living here in July 1890. A child was run over in the road outside, so a newspaper printed her face and this address. A mother’s son was shot, and we get to hear from her in her own words. Nobody ever wrote down an ordinary Tuesday. The archive keeps whatever forced somebody to open a file: a court, a newspaper, a registry, a census. Trouble does that more reliably than anything else does, but it does not do it every time.

Plenty is still blank, and not for want of trouble. We do not know when the lumber yard finally shut, or when 2516 came down, or who Sara Donahue was, or where in Ireland Mary Maloney was born. Those are not vague uncertainties. They are four specific documents that exist somewhere and have not yet been read.

What is settled is the shape of it. Federal ground for thirty-one years. A lumber yard for twenty-eight. Houses from then on, and for ninety-four of those years no man’s name on the deed: Elizabeth Forbes came to it by her husband’s death, Mary Stevens bought it herself, and Agnes Huff held it for thirty-five years, longer than anyone before or since. Seven of the eight are still standing, and yours is the only one of its four that remains a single house on its original lot.

Why every map stops at this street

Every nineteenth-century atlas that reaches South Street stops at its north side, and for most of that run the reason is not laziness. South Street was the city limit. From its founding until the Act of Consolidation of February 2, 1854, the City of Philadelphia was about two square miles, Vine Street to South and river to river, and everything past it was county: thirteen townships, six boroughs, nine districts. This ground was in Passyunk Township. A city atlas had no more reason to draw it than to draw Germantown. Consolidation put the township inside the city, but the habit outlived the boundary by seventy years.

Three of the sheets below are not atlases at all. They are the city’s own manuscript street plans, and they are the exception that proves the rule: they exist precisely because this ground lay outside the city, and somebody had to plot its streets before anyone could build on them.

Not every mapmaker obeyed the line. Smedley crossed it in 1862 and drew the yard; Hopkins crossed it in 1876; Baist crossed it in 1888, and after that everyone did. But the ward atlases, Hexamer & Locher and the Bromleys, ruled the same edge for sixty-four years.

The most famous map ever made of this ward obeyed it too. In 1896 and 1897 W. E. B. Du Bois walked every block of the Seventh Ward for the study published as The Philadelphia Negro, and the map folded into it colors each Black household by class. It runs from Seventh Street to Twenty-fifth and stops at the north curb of South, so this house is off its edge by forty feet, and the 2500 block on the north side is off it too: Du Bois drew nothing west of Twenty-fifth. The color ends before the map does. West of Twenty-second the sheet is almost entirely white, and the last Black households he recorded on South Street are three at Twenty-fourth.

Being township ground had a second effect, visible on every sheet below: city land was cut into building lots, township land was farms and yards. The streets across this block were surveyed and named in 1832 and 1840. In 1855 they were still only lines on a map, with farmland on either side of them. Nobody divided this parcel into house lots until William Forbes did it in March 1888. That is thirty-four years after the city limit moved, and two hundred and five years after Thomas Holme ruled the southern edge of his 1683 plan along Cedar Street and left this ground outside it.

Across the street it went the other way, and far earlier. The north side is inside the old city. A sixteen-inch water main was laid in South Street at this end in 1839, and Hexamer & Locher draw every house on that side in 1858. City water reached the north curb half a century before there was a house on the south side to bring it to. Same street, two regimes, forty feet apart.

One thing here is older than every argument above. Gray’s Ferry Road crosses the bottom of these sheets on the diagonal, ignoring the grid, because it was here first. A ferry ran at its foot from 1673, licensed to Benjamin Chambers after the Swedish settlers upriver complained they were being kept off the Middle Ferry, and in 1696 the colonial government ordered a road laid out from the east landing north to Philadelphia. The Gray brothers widened it in the 1740s and gave it their name, and in April 1789 they dressed the bridge at its foot with laurel arches for Washington on his way to be inaugurated. The streets across this block were not surveyed until 1832 and 1840. The road is older than the grid drawn around it by about a hundred and forty years, and older than your house by two hundred. The surveyors did not break their grid for it. They conceded it.

Which gives everything below one fixed point. South Street and Gray’s Ferry Road cross on every map in this file that reaches this ground, from Hills in 1788 to the aerial photograph of 1995. In that time the city turned Cedar into South, wrote Bainbridge in pencil over Shippen on the 1840 plan, dropped the letter from Fitzwalter, opened Forbes Street and renamed it Bambrey, and never once straightened that road. The triangle it cuts is the one shape you can steer by on all of them.
