How we found it

The working record behind the 2518 South Street timeline — the wrong turns, the withdrawn claims, the searches that came back empty and were worth recording anyway, and the methods that finally worked. None of this belongs in the timeline. All of it is why the timeline can be trusted.

Why this document exists

A timeline that shows its own corrections is hard to read and easy to mistrust — the reader cannot tell which sentence is the finding and which is the retraction. So the timeline states what is known, and flags what is estimated or unproved, and everything about how we got there lives here instead.

Three habits produced most of the errors in this file, and they are worth naming at the top: promoting a name that appears near the parcel into the chain of title; taking a database’s description of its own coverage as the boundary of what exists; and preferring a suggestive coincidence to a dull matching number. Every large mistake below is one of those three.

What is documented: the registry unit book, block 5 S 10, line 160, records MARY STEVENS taking title on December 9, 1924, entered 22 December. That is a property record and it is not in doubt.

What this file went on to say, and should not have: that the 1930 census therefore found her at the head of the household “because she owned the house and they were her lodgers.” Two things in the census cut against that reading, and both were already on this page.

One — the tenure column says RENTED. April 1930, ED 51-100, sheet 10A, line 8: the household is recorded as renting at $60 a month. The enumerator asked whether the home was owned or rented and wrote rented. An owner-occupier does not usually pay rent to herself, and $60 a month is a real figure, not a blank.

Elsewhere on this page I wrote, correctly, that these “are probably the same family and one of the two clerks was careless, but they are not the same word.” I then quietly treated them as the same person here.

1889 – 1890

Why the sale itself will not come up online

A fair question, asked directly: if we can read Henry Maule’s will from the sofa, why can we not read the deed by which he sold the ground your house stands on? The record is not lost. It is a recorded deed, in the Philadelphia Deed Books, indexed by grantor, sitting at 548 Spring Garden Street. There are four separate reasons it will not surface from here.

1. The machine transcription stops just short of it — and you can see exactly where.

Searching the phrase “Bambrey Street” across FamilySearch’s transcribed Philadelphia deeds returns 495 documents. Broken down by decade:
DecadeDocuments naming Bambrey Street
1840s3
1870s159
1880s151
1890s14
A street that generates a hundred and fifty transcribed deed references a decade does not suddenly generate fourteen. That is not Bambrey Street going quiet. That is the transcription ending. The corpus runs richly through the 1880s and falls off a cliff. Your conveyance is dated 1889 or 1890 — on the wrong side of the line by a year or two.
2. Nineteenth-century deeds do not contain street numbers. They are metes and bounds: “on the south side of South Street at the distance of so many feet westward from the west side of Twenty-fifth Street, containing in front or breadth fifteen feet six inches…” Searching “2518 South Street” in the deed corpus returns sixty-nine documents and not one is in Philadelphia — they are addresses in Ohio, Virginia, Illinois, Texas. Even if the right deed had been transcribed, no search on the address could ever find it. You would have to search the geometry.
3. Everything else from that exact moment has a hole in it.
  • The city’s own online deed records begin in 1999. Before 1955 it is paper.
  • The Athenaeum’s architects-and-buildings database holds three records for this block — all odd numbers, all empty stubs.
  • The Real Estate Record and Builders’ Guide, which printed permits with the owner and the builder named, is not digitized for 1889–90.
  • The Inquirer’s real-estate and classified pages are indexed as whole pages, so a search returns the page, not the line — and at that density the page is unreadable.
4. And it may not be one document at all. “Maule sold to a developer” is a modern way of putting it. Small Philadelphia rows went up on ground taken lot by lot, frequently by the builder himself, and very frequently subject to a ground rent rather than bought outright — a Philadelphia speciality. There may be four deeds. There may be one deed and four ground rents. There may be a straw party in the middle. Any of those would still leave Henry Maule, in 1902, owning nothing but the yard.

Which is precisely why the registry unit book is the answer and not the deed index. The unit book is arranged by block and lot, not by name — so it catches every transfer of your ground whatever legal shape it took, and in the order it happened.

What would answer it

  • Registry unit book, block 005S10, City Archives — the one document that cannot be worked around
  • The grantor deed index for Maule, 1888–1892. A grantor index for 1873–78 is already in the transcribed corpus; if a later volume is filmed, the name Maule in 1889 yields a book and page in a single line, and the deed can then be ordered rather than hunted.
  • The building permit, which names owner and builder without needing the deed at all

Reading the schedules again

Five things that were already in the census, unread

Nothing below is a new record. It is all in material already gathered — it had simply never been lined up.

1. Bridget Nolan was a widow with four children, and she married the year this house opened

The 1900 schedule gives Michael and Bridget Nolan as married ten years — so about 1890. It records Bridget as mother of four. And the four other people in the household are not Nolans at all: Mary, James, Elizabeth and Frank Trainor, entered as step-children, aged 26 down to 18, all with Irish-born parents.

So Bridget was Bridget Trainor, an Irishwoman who arrived in 1869, was widowed, raised four children, and around 1890 married Michael Nolan — a Canadian laborer who had reached America in 1885. The first documented tenant of 2518 South Street is John Liveny in July 1890. The Nolans marry in the same year and are in this house by 1891. A new marriage and a new house, more or less together.

And one of them taught school. Mary Trainor, 26, is entered as a school teacher — a professional woman living in a sixteen-foot lodging house on a street with a car barn opposite. Her brothers James and Frank were both stone cutters. This was not a household without prospects.

2. The Huff marriage broke, and Agnes bought the house alone

The 1950 schedule is the one that says it. The household is down to two: Agnes Huff, 49, head, marked separated, working as a cheese packer for a milk company, with her son Joseph, 21, a carton packer. Alexander is not in the house. He is lodging at 2620 South Street, one block west, also marked separated.

And the deed is dated November 5, 1949 — five months before that census. Agnes Huff took title to 2518 South Street in her own name, as a separated woman with a grown son at home, and held it for the next thirty-four years, until she died in 1984. The longest single ownership in this house’s recorded history belongs to her.

Alexander never came back. He died in December 1972 at 304 South 10th Street, still recorded as married, with their son James as informant.

A small coincidence worth noting, not leaning on. Alexander lodged at 2620 South Street in 1950. His daughter Mary had married Francis M. Barrett of 2628 South Street in 1944. Four doors apart on the same block. It may be nothing; it may be that when the marriage ended he went to lodge where his daughter’s people were.

1924–1949 · the last unknown

How we find the day Mary Stevens died

Every other transfer in a hundred and thirty-eight years has a date on it, because every other transfer had a piece of paper signed on a particular morning. This one has no paper. Survivorship happens by operation of law the instant one joint tenant dies — nobody signs, nobody records, and the registry clerk had nothing to write in the date column, which is why he wrote none. So the date we want is not a property record at all. It is a death record. Four routes, best first.

1. The 1949 deed — C.J.P. 2479 p. 221. When Sara Donahue sold to Agnes Huff on November 5, 1949 she had to prove she owned the whole house, and the only proof of that is Mary Stevens’s death. The recital almost certainly says so outright, in the standard form: “…conveyed unto Mary Stevens and Sara Donahue, as joint tenants and not as tenants in common; the said Mary Stevens having since departed this life on the ___ day of ___, whereby title became vested in the said Sara Donahue as survivor.” One document, one sentence, and it is already identified by book and page — the same PhilaDox historical index that gave up the registry book. This is the first thing to pull.

2. Bracket the window before searching any index. Twenty-five years is too many to search blind, and “Mary Stevens” is one of the commonest name pairs in Philadelphia. The 1930 and 1940 censuses at this address will cut it down fast: if Mary Stevens is enumerated here in 1930 and the Huffs are here in 1940, the death falls in a ten-year window instead of a twenty-five-year one. Directory listings for the address do the same job a year at a time.

3. The Pennsylvania death index, 1906–1975 — free, and the state put it online itself. The PHMC scans are open to anyone, no account, no fee: one PDF per year per block of surnames, so a given year is a single file called something like D-28 S.pdf and STEVENS sits a few pages in. They are page images, not text — no search box, no OCR — but they are alphabetical, so each year is a scroll of a minute or two. Every entry carries a county and a certificate number; keep only the Philadelphia ones. The certificate itself, ordered on that number, gives the exact date, the age, the cause, the informant, and — the part that would settle identity beyond argument — the address of the deceased.

4. The death notice. Philadelphia death notices name the street: “STEVENS. — On [date], MARY STEVENS, of 2518 South st.” That single line would do the whole job at once, with no certificate to order — and the Inquirer is searchable full text on ProQuest with a Free Library card. Search the address, not the name. The name returns thousands; 2518 South returns her.

One more record that should exist, and is worth remembering if the first four fail. Pennsylvania taxed a joint tenant’s share on death even when no will was written and no deed was recorded — so there is very likely an inheritance-tax appraisement for Mary Stevens at the Philadelphia Register of Wills, filed within a year of the death, listing this house among her assets. It is an estate record for a woman who may never have had an estate file, and it would be indexed under her name and dated.

What we are actually looking for. Not a transaction — a day. Somewhere between December 1924 and November 1949, one of the two women who had arranged things so the other would never lose the house died, and the arrangement did what it was written to do. Nothing was signed, so nothing was dated, and that is the whole reason this is the last blank line.

Route 4 run, September 1, 2026 — and it comes back empty, which is itself a finding. I swept the whole Inquirer run on ProQuest for “2518 South st” OR “2518 South street”: twenty-six hits across 1860–2001. Fourteen are estate-agent advertisements from 1984 to 2000. Two are marriage-license lists that are OCR misreads of other addresses. Between 1912 and 1984 there is nothing at all. No death notice for Mary Stevens, none for Sara Donahue, none for anyone at this address in the years we care about.

Take that with one caveat. Death-notice columns are the worst OCR on the page — six-point agate, broken type, tight leading — and a notice printed as “2518 South st.” can easily be indexed as 2513 or 25l8. Absence here is weak evidence, not proof. But it does mean route 4 is no longer the cheap one, and the 1949 deed moves up to first by a wide margin.

Stevens · Stevenson · Stephens

The family was not missing, it was misspelled

For weeks Mary Stevens had no life before 1920. Every search for her, her husband, or a Philadelphia marriage came back empty. The cause was not that the records are thin. It is that the surname is written three ways and hers is written two, and every search we ran fixed one spelling and threw away the rest.

The 1900 census sheet says STEVENS. The 1910 census sheet, the same household in the same ward, says STEVENSON. The 1893 marriage register says STEPHENS. Mary is Maloney on her death certificate and Malone on her marriage and on her son’s certificate.

Run any one of those and the family is not there. Run Steven* against Malon* and the whole household falls out at once.

The rule this earns: when an Irish-immigrant family will not surface, the surname is the first suspect, not the archive. Search the stem, not the name — and search the wife under her own variants at the same time, because the two errors compound. The same discipline is still owed to Donahue / Donohue, and to Huff, which has its own set.

Found September 3, 2026, working from John J. Stevens’s 1920 death certificate, which records his mother’s maiden name as Mary Malone — the spelling that opened it.

M. A. Forbes

A landlord who was not a landlord, and a first name that did not survive the hour

“So M. A. Forbes was the landlord?” — no, and the question exposes two things worth being precise about.

One. Every appearance of her on this block is “exr.” She was acting as executrix, a fiduciary selling someone else’s property. If 2518 went unsold, its owner was the estate of William Forbes, not her. She would have been collecting rent on the estate’s behalf. And the evidence that she was “still active in 1897–98” — buying a $150 ground rent in 1893, three judgments as a creditor in 1897 and 1898, all in her own name with no “exr” — shows she had money and property of her own. It says nothing whatever about this house. I let the two run together.

Two. I had been calling her “the widow” and “she” on no evidence at all. “M. A.” is two initials. Widows commonly served as executrices, so I assumed one. I offered a name for her — Margaretta, from a judgment index — and within the hour the same source undercut it. See the note below.

Not was there a landlord — there certainly was. The census schedules record 2518 as rented in 1900, 1910, 1920 and 1930: the Nolans, the Robinsons, the Hollywoods, then Mary Stevenson with the Huffs lodging. Not one of them owned it.

Thirty years of tenants means thirty years of a landlord, and we do not know a single one of their names. That — not the Forbes will, and not the 1894–96 gap — is the open question about who owned this house.

The audit

Where the evidence is thin, and which whole categories of record were never opened

An audit of our own work, rather than of the house. Where the sources are thin, where they are misread, and which whole categories of record we have never once opened.

The one-sentence version

This project is map-rich and deed-poor. We have assembled an unusually good visual record — nine atlases, Sanborns, Hexamer, aerials, land-use maps, photographs — and a thin, patchy set of legal instruments, several of which turn out to describe the neighbors rather than this lot. Almost every open question would be answered by paper, and almost all our effort has gone into pictures.

Fault one

We keep reading the neighborhood and calling it the lot

This is the dominant, systematic error, and it has now happened enough times to be a pattern rather than a run of bad luck. The test is always the same three numbers: 101 feet west of Twenty-fifth, 161 feet of front, 271 feet of depth. Here is every instrument we have claimed, measured against that test.

Everything else is a document about a Maule, or near a Maule, that has been drawn into the chain because it was the nearest thing to hand. That is not a chain of title. It is a family’s paperwork with our lot assumed into it.

Fault one-and-a-half

There is a wall at 1885, and everything we most want is behind it

FamilySearch’s Philadelphia deed books stop around 1885

This is a coverage limit, not a research failure, and it reframes half the plan. Three independent tests, 28 August:

  1. +"fifteen feet six inches" +"Thirtieth Ward" — sixty hits, every one of them 1875–1884. Not one later.
  2. +"Forbes street" +"South street" +"Thirtieth Ward" — zero. Forbes was the name of this block’s side street until about 1895, so every deed on this block between 1885 and 1895 would use it.
  3. A search on the deed phrase “one thousand eight hundred and ninety” returns 245 pages of results — Massachusetts probate, New Jersey deeds, Missouri deeds — and the only Philadelphia items are Will Books. No Philadelphia deed books after 1885 appear at all.

What this puts out of reach from a chair: the sheriff’s deed of 1884–85, the subdivision of the 161 feet into house lots, the builder’s purchase and sales in 1889–90, and every conveyance of this house from then to 1949. All of it is on the far side of the wall. It also explains why the Henry Maule to John C. Wilson deed of 1890 was only ever findable through the grantor index and never in full text.

What is still on this side: Philadelphia wills (1891–92 and 1914–15 both confirmed present), city directories, censuses, newspapers via ProQuest, and everything before 1885 — which is why the Maule years opened up so readily and the house years never will.

The practical consequence — and it was wrong. This box used to say that every remaining question about the house — who subdivided the ground, who built it, who owned it for sixty years — pointed at the City Archives rather than at any search box. All three were answered from a chair, within four days of that sentence being written: the subdivision and the builder out of the Builders’ Guide, the sixty years out of PhilaDox’s historical index. The 1885 wall is real, but it is a wall around FamilySearch, not around the record. The lesson stands in a different form: stop looking in the wrong place — but do not mistake one source’s edge for the edge of what exists.

Fault one-and-three-quarters

The December 1884 results column, tested at last

“Maule” appears once in the Inquirer between 2 and December 6, 1884 — and it is not the sheriff’s sale

Searched 28 August in ProQuest Historical Newspapers, Pennsylvania Collection, using the advanced form’s own date range (Publication date → Specific date range), which works where the pd() command-line syntax silently fails. One result.

It is a probate notice, Philadelphia Inquirer, December 6, 1884, page 7: “Letters testamentary upon the estate of MARY R. MAULE, deceased… [EDWARD] RANDOLPH MAULE… Executors. RICHARD RANDOLPH PARRY.”

Which settles the Edward Randolph Maule question. He and Parry turned up in an 1885 deed as trustees conveying to Hannah R. Maule, and I flagged him as possibly our Edward under another name. He is not. He is an executor of Mary R. Maule’s estate, in a Maule family whose names — Mary R., Hannah R., Edward Randolph — do not overlap ours at any point. Struck from the watch list.

And the negative matters more than the find. If the Inquirer had reported the 1 December sale in a column naming the former owner, “Maule” would have appeared in those five days. It did not. So either the paper did not print results for this sale, or it printed them without the defendant’s name, or the OCR has lost it. The purchaser is not going to fall out of a simple name search.

Two ProQuest mechanics worth keeping. (1) The advanced form’s Specific date range works; pd() on the command line returns zero silently. (2) The Search button only fires when the query has been typed with the keyboard. Setting the field programmatically leaves the page looking correct but the button does nothing — it cost several attempts before I spotted it.

Second search, wider net, same answer — and I am calling this line closed

Search two: sheriff sale South street, 2–December 20, 1884, the whole Pennsylvania Collection. 86 results. Forty-five Pittsburgh Post-Gazette, eleven Daily News, eight Morning Call, six York Daily — and only fourteen Philadelphia Inquirer. The Inquirer hits that surface are classified advertisements: a story about Miss Carrie Gurney of South Thirty-ninth Street, a book published at No. 19 South Ninth Street. Nothing resembling a sheriff’s sale return.

Put beside search one — Maule, 2–6 December, one hit, a different family’s probate notice — the conclusion is no longer tentative. The Inquirer, as digitized by ProQuest, does not carry a results column naming the purchaser at the December 1, 1884 sheriff’s sale.

Three possible reasons, and we cannot distinguish them from here: the paper printed advertisements of sales but not returns; or it printed returns without the defendant’s name; or the broadsheet OCR has simply lost the column. The first is most likely — the 17 November advertisement we already have is exactly the kind of notice that was printed, and its purpose was to attract bidders, not to report afterwards.

So the newspaper route to the purchaser is closed, and the City Archives route — the sheriff’s deed under Keim, or the court file at C.P. No. 2, September Term 1884, No. 225 — is not merely the better option. It is the only one.

The 1870 auction was not advertised as “Naval Asylum” — and this negative is sound

"Naval Asylum", December 15, 1869 – January 20, 1870: zero results.

Then the control, which is what makes it worth recording. The identical phrase across all dates returns 559 results — 489 of them in the Philadelphia Inquirer — spanning 1831 to 2025. The term works. The paper used it constantly. The corpus is deep. So the emptiness of that five-week window is a fact about 1869–70, not about the search.

What follows. M. Thomas & Son did not head their advertisement with the words “Naval Asylum,” or the auction was advertised in a paper other than the Inquirer, or the small-print auction columns of December 1869 have defeated the OCR. Given how badly this corpus handles dense classified type — the same weakness that hid the 1884 sale — the third is a real possibility.

Try instead: "M. Thomas" alone for those weeks, or "Shippen street", or "Philadelphia Exchange". Each is a phrase the advertisement must have contained.

An unexpected lead from the same search. Inquirer, September 22, 1882: Councils debating “the removal of the wall around the Naval Asylum… the beautifying of its grounds… the high Naval Asylum wall.” That is the wall our 1840s and 1850s maps dimension at 48.43, drawn in spaced capitals as MARINE ASYLUM. In 1882 the city was arguing about pulling it down — the same year Hexamer surveyed the car house at the end of this block.

The 1870 auction IS reachable — and the exact page is now identified

The Inquirer reported M. Thomas Exchange sales the following day. Proof, from January 19, 1870, page 3: “Messrs. Thomas & Sons sold at the Exchange, yesterday noon, the…” That is precisely the kind of column the 8 January sale would have produced.

January 8, 1870 was a Saturday. A Saturday noon sale at the Exchange would be reported in the Monday January 10, 1870 issue. That issue is in the database; pages 2, 7 and 8 all surface in a "M. Thomas" search of that week.

So the target is exact: Philadelphia Inquirer, January 10, 1870, the auction and Exchange columns on pages 7 and 8. If the fifteen lots and their purchasers were printed anywhere, they are there — and with them the test of whether T. Abbott bought at that sale.

PAGE 7 CHECKED, 30 August — it is not there, and the reason is instructive. Page 7 of that issue is entirely classified advertising. The M. Thomas & Sons column advertises only forthcoming sales: an assignees’ sale by order of the United States District Court of the National Armor and Shipbuilding Company at Kaighn’s Point, New Jersey, on Tuesday 11 January, and a sale of law books on Wednesday the 12th. No Naval Asylum lots, and nothing retrospective.

Advertisements look forward; reports look back, and they sit in the news pages. The 19 January item that proves the Inquirer reported Exchange sales — “Messrs. Thomas & Sons sold at the Exchange, yesterday noon” — is on page 3, not page 7. So the target looked like page 3 of the January 10, 1870 issue.

PAGE 3 CHECKED TOO — also not there, and this one kills the premise. Page 3 is New York correspondence, foreign notes, hotel arrivals, and a financial column whose only sale report is “Sales at the Stock Exchange, Jan. 8” — New York government bonds and railway shares, followed by cotton and dry-goods markets. There is no Philadelphia real estate column on the page at all.

The reason both pages were empty: Saturday was not a sale day

M. Thomas & Sons’ own advertisement on page 7 says it plainly — “On Tuesday Morning, Jan. 11…” and “On Wednesday Afternoon, Jan. 12…” Philadelphia’s real estate sales at the Exchange were held at Tuesday noon.

Check the item that started this hunt against a calendar. January 1, 1870 was a Saturday, so the 19 January issue carrying “Messrs. Thomas & Sons sold at the Exchange, yesterday noon” was a Wednesday, and its yesterday was Tuesday the 18th. The pattern is exact: sale Tuesday, report Wednesday.

Which means January 8, 1870 — a Saturday — cannot have been an Exchange sale date. Two page requests were spent testing a date that the newspaper’s own schedule rules out. The correct targets are Wednesday issues: 12 January, 19 January, January 26, 1870.

The method lesson, which is the general one: before requesting a page, check what day of the week it was. Institutions ran on weekly rhythms — Exchange sales Tuesday, sheriff’s sales the first Monday, Orphans’ Court on its own day. A date that does not fall on the right weekday is the wrong date, and no amount of page-turning will fix it.

Better still, stop browsing pages and search instead. ProQuest can be date-limited to January–June 1870 and searched for "Naval Asylum" or "Thomas" AND "Shippen street". One search covers every page of every issue; page-stepping covers eight pages of one. Do not re-check pages 3 or 7.

THE TECHNIQUE THAT ACTUALLY WORKS, after a long afternoon of learning it the hard way. (1) Do not fight the advanced form — it re-renders on every dropdown change and silently wipes anything typed. (2) Run one simple search from a freshly loaded advanced page, with the term typed on the keyboard. (3) Then narrow on the results page using the left-panel From / To boxes in yyyy-mm-dd and the Show N results button. That facet is reliable; it took 467 results down to 75 in a single click. (4) Before believing any zero, re-run the same term with All dates as a control.
Still untried in the newspapers. A column headed Sheriff’s Sales or Real Estate Sales searched by heading rather than by name, across 2–December 10, 1884 — and the two other Inquirer databases the Free Library carries, Philadelphia Inquirer Historical Archive and Pennsylvania Historical Newspapers, which have different OCR of the same pages.

Fault two

The same reasoning error, made three times

Each of these was a name that appeared adjacent to this ground in a document, promoted to a link in its chain:

  1. T. Abbott. A name lettered beside Edward Maule on the 1876 atlas. I turned him into the man the Navy bought from in 1826 — on the strength of a newspaper saying the tract “was called” the Abbot lot. He is far more likely a fellow purchaser at the 1870 auction.
  2. Caleb Jones. A carpenter who dealt with the Maules twice in 1871. Written into Part three as the predecessor in title, when no deed of his has ever been shown to describe this lot — and the United States held the ground until eleven months earlier.
  3. “Grace Maule.” An OCR artifact briefly taken for a family member. Caught, but only after being written down.
A fourth version of the same trap, found 28 August — and this one is a search artifact. Searching +"Edward Maule" +"benefit of creditors" returned exactly one hit, in the 1885 deed books, which looked like Edward’s bankruptcy. It is not. Each scanned image is a two-page spread, and FamilySearch indexes the whole spread as one document. “Maule” is on the left page; “benefit of their Creditors” is on the right, in a wholly separate assignment by Henry Crosley and Charles I. Riley. Two unrelated deeds, one image, one false hit.

So a two-term full-text hit proves only that both terms appear somewhere on the spread. Always look at which page each term is on before believing the combination.
The rule this suggests. A name near the parcel is a lead. A name in the description of the parcel is evidence. Nothing should enter the chain on adjacency alone, and if it must be recorded, it belongs in a “people who dealt with this ground” list rather than in the chain.

Fault three

Part two rests almost entirely on one document quoting itself

The federal section reads as our most solid, and in a sense it is — but note how little of it we have seen directly. The Act of Congress of March 3, 1857, the city surveyor’s plan of June 10, 1867, and the auction of January 8, 1870 are all known to us only as recitals inside the 1870 patent. We have not read the Act in the Statutes at Large, the plan at the Streets Department, or the auction advertisement in the newspapers.

A recital is the grantor’s own summary of what came before. It is usually right, and it is exactly where a small error propagates silently down a chain. All three are independently checkable and none has been checked.

Behind that, the 1826 purchase has no deed at all — only Stockton, Gleaves, the 1893 Inquirer and a modern website, all four descending from Edward Shippen’s pamphlet, which we have not read either.

One of these is now resolved — 30 August

“Why did the sheriff come in 1884?” stood unanswered on this file, and a guess that the family was “selling ahead of trouble” was withdrawn here as unevidenced. The trouble is now evidenced. Philadelphia Inquirer, October 24, 1883, page 2, Three Failures: Maule, Bro. & Co., lumber dealers, of Twenty-fifth and South streets, suspended with liabilities of about $150,000, the assigned cause being “its impossibility to realize enough on its assets, which consist largely of real estate, at present unproductive.”

Two collateral gains. First, the same column states that Israel Maule owned “something like a half interest,” died suddenly, and that his widow afterward withdrew her husband’s share in the business — independent corroboration of the February 1879 partition we had reconstructed from deeds 926 and 927 alone, which removes that reconstruction from the “inferred” column. Second, the firm’s address — Twenty-fifth and South — means the 161×271 lot was the lumber yard itself, not a detached investment.

The withdrawn guess was right about the shape and wrong about the date. That is worth recording as a caution in both directions: the retraction was correct on the evidence then held, and the evidence that vindicates it was one search away the whole time.

  1. That the houses went up in 1889–90. TESTED AND WRONG — they went up in 1888, on a dated building permit of 14 May. The old reasoning, kept because the shape of the error is worth seeing: inferred from a gas ordinance, a water main and the first directory tenant. Reasonable, but no dated construction document has ever been seen.
  2. That 2518 is one of the original houses — eight of them, not four, per the permit — and has not been rebuilt or substantially replaced since. Never checked against a permit or a survey.
  3. That the lot has stayed one parcel. Corrected: the 161 feet was subdivided on March 28, 1888 into thirty-eight lots — eight fronting South Street and thirty on the new Forbes Street. The fifty-nine year hole — CLOSED

1890 to 1949 — what was searched, what failed, and what finally worked

Closed on September 1, 2026, and this section is now a record of how, not a plan

Everything below this box was written while the hole was open, and it is kept because the failures are instructive. What closed it was the registry unit book for block 5 S 10, line 160 — a volume this file had carried for weeks as a City Archives errand, and which turns out to be online and free inside PhilaDox’s historical index. Elizabeth A. Forbes and others by will (1890) → Mary Stevens (Dec 9, 1924) → Stevens and Donahue as joint tenants → Sara Donahue by survivorship → Agnes M. Huff (Nov 5, 1949).

The mistake worth naming. This file recorded PhilaDox as covering 1974 onward, because that is what the search screen says. That sentence describes what is certified, not what exists. I took a system’s self-description as the boundary of the record, and it cost weeks. The owner of the house found the historical index in about twenty minutes.

One date was still missing, and only one: Mary Stevens’s death, which is when the survivorship operated. There is no deed to find — survivorship needs none — so it was a death record, not a property record.

FOUND, September 2, 2026 — April 27, 1949. The free PHMC death indexes were downloaded whole (thirteen volumes, 2,793 page images, no text layer), machine-read, and searched on the Soundex code S315, which is where Stevens, Stephens, Stevenson and Stephenson all collapse to one entry. That gave a certificate number; the certificate itself was read free through Ancestry Pennsylvania, which the Commonwealth provides to residents. She died at 2518 South Street, and her daughter signed the register. Method note for the next hard search: when a scanned index is Soundex-ordered, spelling variants stop being a problem and become the point.

Two long-running conflicts closed the same day

The name. This file carried Stephens (1920 census), Stevenson (1930 census) and Stevens (registry, deeds, death notices) as an open question for weeks, and at one point argued that “Stevens and Stevenson are not the same word.” Her own death certificate names her husband as “the late James Stevens.” The property records were right throughout; both enumerators guessed, and both guessed wrong.

What would actually close it

  1. Philadelphia tax assessment ledgers, City Archives. The one record that answers all fifty-nine years at once. Superseded. The registry unit book did the job, online and free. The ledgers remain the way to find occupancy and value year by year, which the registry does not give — but they are no longer needed to name the owners.
  2. The deed recitals you may already hold. A Pennsylvania deed recites the conveyance before it. The 1949 deed — C.J.P. 2479 p. 221, Sara Donahue to the Huffs — will recite how Sara Donahue got the house, which is a direct step backwards into the hole from its far end. If the 1949 deed is in the settlement papers, read its recital first.
  3. The 1940 census, free at the National Archives. This file records the Huffs as “owners by 1940” while the deed is 1949. I called that a discrepancy; it is not one. The owner of the house corrected me: an articles of agreement sale — Philadelphia’s ordinary installment purchase — gives possession and an honest “owned” census answer years before the deed passes. The two records agree; together they are the evidence.

Fitzwater is not a misspelling on the old plans, and neither is Fitzwalter. The name is a Norman patronymic, Fitz Walter, son of Walter; Fitzwalter is the full form and Fitzwater the contraction. The street is named for Thomas Fitzwater, a Quaker who came over with Penn and died in 1699, and his own family used the short form: they gave their name to Fitzwatertown in Montgomery County, which has never been spelled any other way. So Thomson lettering Fitzwalter in 1832 is a surveyor reaching for the formal version, the same instinct that had him write Cedar when the city was already saying South. The story that a clerk misspelled it and nobody fixed it is an unsourced comment on a blog, and it runs backwards to the family’s own usage.

Other names now worth searching in their own right: Moyamensing, Fitzwalter, Grays Ferry Road, Bank St., Water St., Ashton, Grover, Sutherland, Alexander.

The 26th Ward — what it holds and what it does not

The ward this ground sat in from 1854 to about 1866. Explored as far as the internet reaches:

  • Hexamer & Locher 1858–60 — no 26th Ward volume exists. See above.
  • Streets Department plans — Twenty-sixth Ward returns 28, mostly Dickinson and Tasker Streets, deep South Philadelphia. Two are genuinely this ward; neither reaches this block. The 1864 curb-height plan (099-11) is a triangle that stops short of it.
  • The ward atlases — J. L. Smith’s 26th & 36th Wards (1903) and E. V. Smith’s 26th, 36th & 48th (1928) are both too late: by then this ground was the 30th.
  • The deeds — +"Maule" +"Twenty sixth Ward" returns 56, and they show the family dealing across the whole ward, not only at Twenty-fifth and South. Gray’s Ferry Road — where the dates in the maps introduction come from

The claim that the road predates the grid is not ours; it is on the record, and these are the citations.

  • 1673 — the Lower Ferry, or Chambers’ Ferry, established at this point on the Schuylkill by Benjamin Chambers, licensed after Swedish settlers complained of being kept off the Middle Ferry. Ferries, Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia.
  • 1696 — the colonial government directs two roads laid out from either end of the Lower Ferry: one from the east landing north to Philadelphia, one west toward Darby, the latter becoming part of the King’s Highway to Baltimore and the southern colonies. Same source.
  • 1740s — George and Robert Gray inherit the crossing, buy about 199 acres on both banks, widen the road and build the tavern and gardens that give it the name.
  • April 20, 1789 — Washington crosses on his way to be inaugurated; the Grays dress the bridge with laurel arches and the flags of the states, the display staged by Charles Willson Peale.

What we have not verified is that the modern Grays Ferry Avenue follows the exact alignment ordered in 1696. The sources establish descent, not identity, and the introduction is worded as descent. The comparison that matters is safe either way: the streets of this block were not surveyed until the Thomson plan of 1832 and the Kneass plan of 1840, both of which are in the map gallery, and both of which draw the road already in place and work around it.

One correction made in drafting: the road and Cedar Street do not appear on every map in the file. Holme in 1683 stops at the city and shows neither. The claim as written is “every map in this file that reaches this ground,” which runs from Hills in 1788.

A pale rectangle east of the row, 1965 to 1970 — UNRESOLVED

All seven DVRPC aerials were registered to one another and cropped to the same ground: the sixty-three feet of 2516–2522 on the south side of South Street, east of Bambrey. The four houses cannot be counted. Each is about twenty-one pixels of original photograph, and in no frame can four be told from three. Contrast was normalized across all seven with adaptive histogram equalization, and consecutive years were differenced to isolate change; neither recovered the row. The limit is spatial resolution, not tone, so there is no point re-running any enhancement on these images. The demolition of 2516 is not datable from them.

One thing did change at a larger scale. On the ground east of the row, in 1965 the surface reads dark and open with no hard edges; from 1970 onward there is a large pale rectangle with sharp corners, present in every later frame. Three readings, and we cannot choose between them:

  • Something was built or paved on the old yard ground between 1965 and 1970. The owner’s recollection is that nothing stood between the lumber yard and the 2013 condominium, which counts against this.
  • It is the Thornton-Fuller Auto Co. building, which the 1950 Sanborn shows on the far side of the line of Twenty-fifth Street, lettered fire proof construction, built 1925. If our east-west bearing is off by a block, this is simply a building we already knew about.
  • It is an artifact of exposure. The 1965 and 1970 flights differ markedly in contrast, and a flat pale roof can drop into the surrounding tone in one and stand out in the other.

The second reading is the most likely, because the bearing and the feet-per-pixel used to place the row were both derived rather than surveyed. Do not put this in the timeline as a finding. What would settle it is a frame in which the line of Twenty-fifth Street is unambiguous.

Where the Pemberton chain actually comes from — and how to break it open

Everything we have about the Penns, Masters, Kinsey and the Pembertons descends from one pamphlet: a historical sketch of the Naval Asylum by Medical Director Edward Shippen, U.S.N., written before 1886. Four later accounts all quote it. Nobody documents a Pemberton-to-Abbot sale. These are the things that would settle it.

A · Shippen’s pamphlet itself — the single most valuable item on this page

Stockton calls it “the only printed matter upon the subject.” Try HathiTrust, Google Books and Internet Archive for author Shippen, Edward with Naval Asylum, and the Library Company and HSP catalogs for a Philadelphia pamphlet. Probable date 1878–1885.

https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/ls?q1=%22naval%20asylum%22%20shippen;a=srchls;anyall1=phrase;field1=ocr;lmt=ft

B · Hills, 1808 — does the tract say Pemberton or Abbot?

My earlier link to this was dead and I should not have given it to you. I built view-image.cfm/237-MP-026 by copying the pattern from the atlases. It is not a real URL for this item. 237-MP-026 is a purchase-only Athenaeum item — its resource page offers a Purchase button and nothing else, so there is no free flat image to open.

But the same map is free as tiles in the interactive viewer, under the layer id Hills1808. That is the one to use.

This is the direct test, and it is one screenshot. Hills surveyed the environs in the summers of 1801–07 and letters country seats with their owners’ names. Find the east bank of the Schuylkill between Cedar Street and Grays Ferry Road and read what is written on it. If it says Pemberton, the family still held it in 1808 and the sale came after James died in 1809. If it says Abbot, the sale was earlier and Phoebe is not the grantor.

https://www.philageohistory.org/tiles/viewer/?layers=Hills1808,GoogleHybridLabels&c=39.94350,-75.18220&z=16

If that opens somewhere odd, just open the viewer plain and tick 1808 Phila and Vicinity in the Layers panel, then use the address search. There is a second version of the same survey, Hills1808a (“1808 Phila (v2)”) — worth flipping between them, since one scan is often cleaner. Maximum zoom on both is 17. The camera button in the left toolbar exports what you are looking at.

Also free in the viewer, layer Scull1750. This is the decade John Kinsey died and the estate would have changed hands. It is a small-scale map and may name only the biggest seats, but it costs one click.

HSF.D2D3 is Paxton 1810; GR.II.05 is Paxton 1811; GR.II.08 is McClure and Tanner’s Chart of the River Schuylkill, 1828 — two years after the Navy bought, and a river chart names wharf owners along the bank, which matters because the purchase included wharves.

E · James Pemberton’s will, 1809

Philadelphia Register of Wills. If the Plantation was still his at death, the will disposes of it and names who took it. Try FamilySearch full text with +"James Pemberton" +"Plantation" and +"Phoebe Pemberton" restricted to 1809–1830.

Do not repeat these. Stockton’s 1886 Proceedings history, Gleaves’ 1931 Proceedings history, the Asylum Projects site history and the April 30, 1893 Inquirer column have all been read in full. They agree because they share a source, not because they confirm each other. Reading a fifth retelling will add nothing.

Your own deed, and how to walk the chain backwards for nothing

Tested and working, 31 August. The City of Philadelphia’s Atlas has a Deeds tab that lists every recorded document for a parcel and links straight to it. View-only, watermarked copies are free — you only pay if you want a clean printable one.

https://atlas.phila.gov/2518%20South%20St/deeds

It lists thirteen documents for this house. The one you want first is document 53031008 — DEED, March 1, 2016, Edelblut to McGrane. Click it, accept the Department of Records disclaimer once, and the image loads.

The trick is the recital, and it is how title searchers actually work. Near the end of every Pennsylvania deed is a paragraph beginning “BEING the same premises which…” It names the previous deed — grantor, date, and book and page or document number.

So each deed is a pointer to the one before it. Open the 2016 deed, read its recital, and it will name the 2000 deed (Lyon to Edelblut, document 50125135). Open that one, read its recital, and it will name how Lynne Lyon got the house — which is a documented step into the 1984–2000 gap, for free, from a chair.

Keep going until the recitals point at something Atlas does not hold.

PhilaDox — for anything older than December 1999

https://epayss.phila-records.com/web/

Checked September 1, 2026: this is the live address, and it is the same site the Atlas document links open into. It lands on a disclaimer page with an “I Accept” button — and a reCAPTCHA “I’m not a robot” checkbox above it that keeps the button grayed out until it is ticked. Confirmed by looking at it, September 1, 2026.

That bot check is the actual barrier, and it is one I will not go through — not a preference, a hard line. It takes you two seconds and then the site stays accepted.

Once inside, it searches by name, by address, and by book and page — and its index runs further back than the Atlas display. The document to ask it for first is:

Deed Book JTD 1048, page 10 — recorded June 2, 1999, dated May 12, 1999. Frank O. Gould and Douglas Frenkel to Lynne Lyon.

Read its recital paragraph — “BEING the same premises which…” — and it names whoever sold to Gould and Frenkel, with another book and page. Then repeat. Each deed is a rung, and in principle they run back to Sara Donahue in 1949 and beyond her into the fifty-nine year hole.
Where it will stop, and why. Atlas says so itself, in plain words on the page: “the following list shows documents recorded from December 1999 forward, and may not be a complete history of title for the parcel.”

So Atlas holds the documents from 1999; the recitals inside them reach back further. When a recital names a deed too old for Atlas, that citation — book and page — is exactly what you hand to the Department of Records, or to a title company, or to whoever goes to the City Archives. A book-and-page citation is worth ten times a name.

The registry number this file has been carrying as an errand is confirmed on the same page: 005S100160, parcel area 1,040 square feet, perimeter 156 feet.

The Builders’ Guide — where the 1888 documents are, page by page

Everything found on 31 August about William Forbes comes from one publication: the Philadelphia Real Estate Record and Builders’ Guide, a trade weekly that printed every conveyance and every building permit in the city from 1886. The whole run is free at the Internet Archive — no card, no login, no subscription — and every page is a scan you can read on screen and screenshot.

https://archive.org/details/PhilaBuildersGuide_v3_1888

Why this source was worth more than everything else tried this week. The Guide reports the same deeds that FamilySearch stops carrying at 1885, and it reports the permits that only exist on paper at the City Archives. It walks straight through the 1885 wall. Volumes run to at least 1929, so every conveyance of this house between 1886 and the 1920s should be in here — and that is the obvious next sweep.
